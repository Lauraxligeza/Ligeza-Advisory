Market adaptation · 30 November 2025 · 5 min read

Content adaptation starts with the commercial context

Translation quality matters, but it cannot resolve a weak market assumption, an unclear value proposition or missing evidence.

Ligeza Advisory Insights

Commercial question

What must a target market buyer understand and believe before taking the next step and does the current content provide enough relevant evidence?

Why it matters

Teams often begin adaptation with a source page and a language pair. That is necessary production information, but it does not explain whether the source page is strategically suitable for the new market. The offer may occupy a different competitive position, use unfamiliar category terminology or ask for a level of commitment the page does not support.

Evidence to examine

  • How relevant competitors describe the category and structure comparable offers
  • The search terms and questions associated with the intended purchase
  • What information buyers seek at the price point and level of risk involved
  • Payment, delivery, service and regulatory expectations that affect the journey
  • Customer research, sales objections and behavioural data from the target segment

Analysis

The useful distinction is not between “literal translation” and a supposedly universal cultural style. It is between content based on evidence and content based on assumptions. Adaptation can involve terminology, examples, proof, page order, calls to action, interface details or the offer itself. Which changes matter depends on the category, segment and purchasing context.

A lower conversion rate after launch does not prove that culture or copy is the cause. Traffic quality, price, product market fit, technical performance and fulfilment can produce the same symptom. Diagnosis should compare plausible explanations before prescribing new messaging.

Limitations and uncertainties

Public competitor pages show what companies publish, not necessarily what drives customer decisions. Search data indicates demand language, not purchase motivation. Interviews reveal reported preferences, which may differ from behaviour. Strong analysis uses several evidence types and states where the conclusion remains provisional.

Practical implications

  1. Define the decision

    Write down the action the content must help a specific segment take.

  2. Audit assumptions

    Mark each claim about the market as supported, likely, unknown or requiring a test.

  3. Compare the journey

    Review the source experience against relevant target market alternatives.

  4. Prioritise changes

    Adapt the elements most likely to affect comprehension, credibility or action, then measure the result.

What to examine next

Start with the part of the journey closest to the commercial problem: the acquisition message, landing page, product information, checkout or sales handover. A focused review usually produces more useful evidence than rewriting every asset at once.
